About This Simulation

This interactive model recreates the core computation that early electronic computers like ENIAC performed: solving differential equations of projectile motion using numerical integration. Scroll down for detailed historical and mathematical notes with references.

The Problem: Firing Tables

During World War II, every artillery piece required a firing table — a booklet of pre-calculated trajectories telling gunners what angle to set for a given target distance, wind speed, air density, and elevation. A single firing table for one weapon could contain thousands of trajectories, and each individual trajectory required the numerical solution of a second-order nonlinear differential equation.[1]

The U.S. Army's Ballistic Research Laboratory (BRL) at Aberdeen Proving Ground, Maryland, was the centre of this effort. By 1943, the backlog of uncomputed firing tables was so severe that new weapons were being shipped to the front without them — gunners had to estimate trajectories by trial and error, with lethal consequences for accuracy.[2]

The Human Computers

Before electronic machines, "computers" were people. The BRL employed approximately 100 women — many recruited from mathematics departments at colleges like the University of Pennsylvania — who worked in teams using desk calculators to solve trajectory equations by hand. Each trajectory required roughly 750 individual multiplications, and a single trajectory took about 40 hours of human labour to complete. The Bush differential analyser, an analog mechanical computer, could reduce this to about 15 minutes, but the machine was imprecise and frequently broke down.[3]

Six of these women "computers" — Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Meltzer, Fran Bilas, and Ruth Lichterman — were later selected to become the first programmers of ENIAC, translating the very equations they had calculated by hand into wired machine instructions.[4]

Equations of Motion

The fundamental problem is Newton's second law applied to a projectile moving through air. The projectile experiences two forces: gravity (constant, downward) and aerodynamic drag (opposing the direction of motion, proportional to the square of speed). In two dimensions, the equations of motion are:[9]

m · (d²x/dt²) = −D · (dx/dt) ... horizontal m · (d²y/dt²) = −D · (dy/dt) − m·g ... vertical where D = ½ · ρ · Cd · A · |v| and |v| = √( (dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)² )

Here m is the projectile mass, ρ is air density (which varies with altitude — a complication the full BRL calculations accounted for), Cd is the drag coefficient (dependent on Mach number, shell shape, and spin), A is the cross-sectional area, and g is gravitational acceleration.

Why Derivatives Matter

The entire problem is expressed in terms of derivatives — rates of change. Position's first derivative with respect to time is velocity; velocity's first derivative is acceleration. The drag force itself depends on velocity, making the equation nonlinear: you cannot solve it with a simple closed-form formula as you can for basic projectile motion without air resistance.

This is the key insight: without drag, the trajectory is a perfect parabola and can be solved on paper with algebra. With drag, you get a coupled nonlinear system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) that can only be solved numerically — step by step, computing the derivative at each point to find the next point. This is exactly what the dashed "no-drag" reference curve and the solid "with-drag" computed curve in the simulation illustrate.

The Role of the First and Second Derivatives

At each integration step, the computer must evaluate:

1st derivative of position → velocity: vₓ = dx/dt, vᵧ = dy/dt 1st derivative of velocity → acceleration: aₓ = dvₓ/dt = −(k·|v|)·vₓ aᵧ = dvᵧ/dt = −g − (k·|v|)·vᵧ where k = D/m = (ρ·Cd·A) / (2m) The trajectory's curvature (d²y/dx²) indicates how sharply the path bends — visible in the readout panel as the shell arcs downward.

In the simulation's live readout, dy/dx shows the slope of the trajectory (the first derivative of the path curve), and d²y/dx² shows how rapidly that slope is changing (the curvature). When dy/dx passes through zero, the projectile has reached its apex. When d²y/dx² grows large and negative, the shell is diving steeply — this is when drag has its greatest effect on the descent trajectory.

Simplifications in This Simulation

The real BRL calculations included additional factors that this simulation omits for clarity: altitude-dependent air density (the atmosphere gets thinner higher up), the Coriolis effect from Earth's rotation, Mach-dependent drag coefficients (drag changes dramatically around the speed of sound), wind profiles at different altitudes, and the Magnus effect from projectile spin. The BRL's "Siacci method" and later the "3-degree-of-freedom" and "6-degree-of-freedom" models added these corrections progressively.[10]

Euler's Method — The Simplest Numerical Integrator

This simulation uses Euler's method (also called the forward Euler method), the most elementary technique for numerically solving ordinary differential equations. Given a current state (position, velocity) and the ability to compute the derivative (acceleration), we simply step forward by a small time increment Δt:[11]

Given: y'(t) = f(t, y) (the ODE) y(t₀) = y₀ (initial condition) Euler step: y(t + Δt) ≈ y(t) + Δt · f(t, y(t)) Applied to ballistics: vₓ(t+Δt) = vₓ(t) + aₓ(t) · Δt vᵧ(t+Δt) = vᵧ(t) + aᵧ(t) · Δt x(t+Δt) = x(t) + vₓ(t) · Δt y(t+Δt) = y(t) + vᵧ(t) · Δt

The method is intuitive: evaluate the slope (derivative) at the current point, then walk in that direction for a small step. Repeat. The green dots on the trajectory plot represent these discrete computation steps — you can see how the computer "feels its way" along the curve, one step at a time.

Step Size and Accuracy

The Δt slider in the simulation controls step size. Try changing it: with a very large Δt (0.50s), the trajectory becomes visibly coarser and less accurate. With a tiny Δt (0.01s), the result is smooth and precise but requires many more computation steps. Euler's method has first-order accuracy, meaning halving the step size halves the error per step (but doubles the number of steps and thus the total computation time).[11]

This trade-off was deeply practical for the early computers. Each arithmetic operation took real time — about 200 microseconds for a multiplication on ENIAC. Choosing the right Δt was a balance between accuracy requirements (gunners needed trajectories correct to within a few metres) and the hours of machine time available.[1]

Better Methods Used Historically

While Euler's method illustrates the principle, the BRL and subsequent computing centres typically used higher-order methods for production calculations:

Runge-Kutta (4th order, "RK4"): ───────────────────────────────── k₁ = Δt · f(tₙ, yₙ) k₂ = Δt · f(tₙ + Δt/2, yₙ + k₁/2) k₃ = Δt · f(tₙ + Δt/2, yₙ + k₂/2) k₄ = Δt · f(tₙ + Δt, yₙ + k₃) yₙ₊₁ = yₙ + (k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄) / 6 Error per step: O(Δt⁵) — far more accurate than Euler for the same step size.

The Runge-Kutta method evaluates the derivative at four points within each step, effectively "sampling" the curve more thoroughly. This was the workhorse method for ballistic computation from the 1940s onward and remains widely used today.[12] The Adams-Bashforth and Adams-Moulton predictor-corrector methods were also employed, reusing derivative evaluations from previous steps to improve efficiency.[11]

What "Solving with Derivatives" Actually Means

When we say early computers "used derivatives to solve missile problems," the precise meaning is this: at each moment in time, the computer evaluates the forces acting on the projectile (gravity, drag) to compute the rate of change (derivative) of velocity. It then uses that derivative to update velocity, and uses velocity (the derivative of position) to update position. The entire trajectory emerges from this chain of derivative evaluations — thousands of tiny steps, each dependent on the last.

ENIAC (1945)

The Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer was the first general-purpose electronic digital computer. Designed by J. Presper Eckert and John Mauchly at the University of Pennsylvania's Moore School of Electrical Engineering, it was funded by the U.S. Army specifically to compute ballistic firing tables.[1]

ENIAC contained 17,468 vacuum tubes, 7,200 crystal diodes, 1,500 relays, 70,000 resistors, 10,000 capacitors, and roughly 5 million hand-soldered joints. It weighed 27 tonnes, occupied 167 square metres, and consumed 150 kilowatts of power. It could perform about 5,000 additions per second — roughly 1,000 times faster than the electromechanical relay calculators that preceded it.[1]

ENIAC was programmed by physically rewiring its patch cables and setting its 3,000 function-table switches. "Programming" a new problem could take days of recabling. The six women programmers (the "ENIAC girls") had to understand both the differential equations and the machine's physical architecture to translate mathematics into wire connections.[4]

Before ENIAC: Analog Computers

Before digital electronic computing, the BRL relied on Vannevar Bush's differential analyser (1931) — a room-sized mechanical analog computer that used rotating shafts, gears, and wheel-and-disc integrators to solve differential equations continuously. The output was drawn directly as a curve on paper by a pen attached to the mechanism. The analyser could solve a trajectory in about 15 minutes, but its accuracy was limited to about 0.1%, and it required physical reconfiguration (changing gear trains) for each new equation.[5]

The key conceptual difference: analog computers represented mathematical quantities as physical measurements (shaft rotations, voltages), computing derivatives through the physical process of integration. Digital computers like ENIAC represented numbers as discrete digits and computed derivatives through arithmetic — subtraction, multiplication, and division — step by step. The digital approach was slower per operation but far more precise and flexible.[13]

After ENIAC: The Stored-Program Revolution

ENIAC's successor at the BRL was EDVAC (Electronic Discrete Variable Automatic Computer, 1949), which implemented John von Neumann's stored-program architecture — instructions and data shared the same memory, eliminating the need for physical rewiring. This made changing from one trajectory problem to another a matter of minutes rather than days.[14] The stored-program concept, outlined in von Neumann's famous 1945 "First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC," was directly inspired by his work with ENIAC on ballistic and nuclear calculations.[7]

From Firing Tables to General Computing

The trajectory calculation problem was the original "killer app" that justified the enormous expense of building the first electronic computers. But once these machines existed, their generality became apparent. ENIAC's very first real computation was not a firing table but a simulation of nuclear chain reactions for the hydrogen bomb, run in late 1945 at the request of Los Alamos physicist Edward Teller.[7]

The numerical methods developed for ballistic computation — finite difference schemes, Runge-Kutta integration, predictor-corrector algorithms — became the foundation of computational science as a whole. The same techniques are used today in weather forecasting, structural engineering, orbital mechanics, fluid dynamics, and countless other fields where differential equations must be solved numerically.[12]

The Derivative as a Computational Primitive

What made these early computers revolutionary was not that they could add or multiply — desk calculators could do that. It was that they could chain together thousands of arithmetic operations fast enough to approximate continuous mathematical processes. The derivative — the instantaneous rate of change — is inherently a concept about continuity and limits. A digital computer can never compute a true derivative; it can only approximate one through finite differences. But by making those approximations fast enough and with small enough steps, the results become indistinguishable from exact solutions for all practical purposes.

This philosophical shift — from exact analytical solutions to "good enough" numerical approximations — was one of the most important intellectual transitions in 20th-century applied mathematics. The ballistic computing programme was where that transition first happened at scale, and it reshaped how science and engineering engage with differential equations to this day.[15]

Guided Missiles and Control Theory

As rocketry advanced through the 1950s, the problem evolved from predicting trajectories (firing tables for unguided shells) to controlling them in real time (guided missiles). This required solving the same differential equations not once for a table, but continuously during flight — computing derivatives of position from radar tracking data, comparing the actual trajectory to the desired one, and sending correction signals. This real-time derivative computation was the birth of modern digital control theory and led directly to the guidance computers used in ICBMs, the Apollo programme's spacecraft navigation, and today's autonomous systems.[16]
