—Colonization: how to control people at a distance.
—Rise of industralialization, modernization: accumulation and deployment of capital. Fixed vs variable costs: factories, rail.
—Early informatics: the printing press, the typewriter. Babbage's "Difference Engine" (1830s)

Thought experiment: Throwing a Stone

So let's throw a stone; we have essentially (simplistically) 4 variables:

Can be done by hand, but each angle + velocity needs a new set of values...

New problems: mass, shape of stone (etc. etc)

1angle of release
2velocity (metres/yards per second)
3gravity (wants to pull the stone back to ground)
4drag (air resistance; varies with velocity, meaning we need to calculate as we go - not an analytic solution)
5So we now need fancy (17th century and beyond) math:
—Euler's method (following Newton / Leibniz): decompose the entire stone's thrown into a sequence:
—Step 1: x, y, velocity x, velocity y
—Step 2: new x, new y -> then new velocity x, new velocity y
—etc...
6Euler: 1760s
7Runge–Kutta Corrections: 1890s/1900s
8Initial computation: Euler method (1940s); Runge–Kutta (late 1940s/50s) ...

1Much of AI-related - and general - computing from 1940s to 2020s involves mapping continuous (derivates) into discrete (very small differences). At least as far as simulation (from ballistics to language generation) are concerned.
2Note analog (non digital) calculators can compute continuities directly! Arguably what biological brains also do - open for debate.
